You'll hear the race before you see it. A crowd stands along a rope on open steppe, shading their eyes and looking towards a low ridge. For a long time there is nothing but wind and the murmur of people waiting. Then a smudge of dust appears on the horizon, and a cheer rolls down the line as the first horses come into view, galloping hard, their small riders crouched forward and calling them on. Families shout the names of horses they know. Someone near you is already on the phone to a relative. Within a minute the leaders have flashed past and the crowd is surging towards the finish to see who won.

That is Naadam: part national celebration, part country fair, part sporting obsession. It is the festival most visitors to Mongolia want to see, and it is worth planning a summer trip around. This guide explains what happens, where to watch, and how to go about it.

## What Naadam is

Naadam is Mongolia's national festival. UNESCO, which inscribed it on its [Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/naadam-mongolian-traditional-festival-00395) in 2010, describes it as a festival celebrated every year from 11 to 13 July across Mongolia, focused on three traditional games: horse racing, wrestling and archery.

The word is often translated simply as "games", and the festival is also known locally as the "three games of men". The sport is the heart of it, but UNESCO's description makes clear that it is about much more: ceremonial praise for the competitors, distinctive costumes and equipment, and music and performing arts, including long song, khöömei overtone singing, bie biyelgee dance and the morin khuur, the horsehead fiddle. UNESCO also notes that everyone is allowed and encouraged to take part, which is one reason the festival feels so communal.

The national festival in Ulaanbaatar marks the 1921 revolution. Steppe & Sky's [festivals page](https://steppeandsky.tourground.com/festivals) notes that the official public holiday commonly runs from 11 to 15 July, and that local naadams are held through the month on dates that vary each year.

## The three games

### Wrestling

Wrestling, or bökh, is the event that fills the stadium. Pairs of wrestlers in the traditional costume, a tight open-fronted vest and short trunks, grapple on the grass with no time limit. According to the [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naadam) of the rules, a wrestler loses when any part of his body other than his feet or hands touches the ground.

Watch what happens after each bout: the winner performs the eagle dance, arms spread like wings. Each wrestler has an encourager, called a zasuul, who sings his praises during the tournament. Wrestlers who go deep into the competition earn titles named after creatures of strength and speed, among them the kestrel, the elephant and the lion, and the crowd knows the famous names.

It is a slow-burning, absorbing sport once you understand it. Bouts can be over in seconds or go on for a long time as two big men lean into each other, looking for a grip.

### Horse racing

The races are where Naadam leaves the stadium for the steppe. Unlike the short sprints of Western racing, Naadam races are long cross-country gallops, with the distance set by the age of the horses. The jockeys are children, who train through the months before the festival, and the races are understood as a test of the horse as much as the rider.

Before the start, the audience sings traditional songs and the jockeys sing a song of their own, and prizes and titles go to the leading horses as well as to the riders. In Ulaanbaatar, the races are held outside the city at Khui Doloon Khudag, according to Steppe & Sky's festivals page, which means a separate trip from the stadium and an early start.

### Archery

Archery is the quietest of the three games and, for many visitors, the most surprising. Rows of archers in traditional dress take turns to shoot at low targets set out on the grass, and each hit is sung out, a sound Steppe & Sky's festivals page lists among the highlights of the day. UNESCO's description of Naadam speaks of sportsmen, sportswomen and children competing, and the archery ground is a good place to see that range. In the capital, the archery ground is next to the stadium.

## The capital or the country?

You have two broad choices, and many travellers try to do both.

**The national Naadam in Ulaanbaatar** has the spectacle. The opening ceremony in the National Sports Stadium brings horsemen, banners, music and dancers, and the stadium wrestling draws the biggest names. It is also crowded and needs planning. Steppe & Sky's festivals page notes that tickets for the opening ceremony sell out and are best arranged well ahead, and that the capital is at its busiest around Naadam, so flights and accommodation should be booked early.

**A local naadam** in a provincial or district centre has the intimacy. Through July, aimag and soum centres hold their own festivals, often on different dates, and these are smaller and more relaxed. You can stand close to the wrestling, wander between the food stalls and watch the horses come in from the horizon with the people who trained them. The catch is that local dates are set each year, sometimes at short notice, so you need flexibility in your plans and someone on the ground who can find out what is happening and when.

## Food and the festival atmosphere

Away from the competitions, Naadam is a holiday. Families gather, friends meet and the stalls around the venues do a steady trade. The dish everyone associates with the festival is khuushuur, a fried pastry filled with meat, eaten hot in the hand. You may also be offered airag, fermented mare's milk, which is traditionally served at the festival; take a small sip if you are unsure, and it is fine to say you have had enough.

Look out too for the shagai, the knucklebones used for games and fortune-telling, and for the long-song singers and morin khuur players who are part of the festival's music.

## Staying safe in the crowds

Naadam brings very large crowds, and the FCDO's [safety advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) is clear, at the time of writing (November 2026), that pickpockets may operate in markets and crowded places and that the risk increases during Tsagaan Sar and Naadam. It suggests you:

- keep your phone, passport and money in a secured bag on your person;
- leave valuables in a hotel safe if possible;
- avoid being out alone at night in Ulaanbaatar, and ask your hotel or restaurant to order a car, use licensed taxis or use the trackable UBCab app.

The FCDO also advises carrying your passport at all times, as police will not accept a copy.

## The July weather

July is the warmest month and often the most changeable. The FCDO [notes](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) that summer temperatures can reach +35°C and that weather conditions often change without warning. Bring sun protection, a hat and water for long hours at an outdoor venue, and a light waterproof for the afternoon downpours. If you are heading to a race on the steppe, expect dust and wind as well. ## Practical planning

- **Confirm the dates for your year.** The national festival is fixed around 11 July, but the length of the holiday and the dates of local naadams can change.
- **Book early.** Flights, hotels and drivers in Ulaanbaatar are in high demand in July.
- **Check your entry rules.** For British citizens, the FCDO's [entry requirements page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/entry-requirements) says, at the time of writing, that you can visit for tourism for up to 30 days without a visa until 31 December 2026, and that your passport must be valid for at least six months after you arrive. Other nationalities should check their own government's advice.
- **Allow for travel disruption.** The FCDO's [warnings page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/warnings-and-insurance) reports fuel shortages that may affect flights and road travel. Leave some slack in your schedule.
- **Ask before you photograph.** Competitors and families are generally used to cameras at Naadam, but ask before taking close portraits, and remember the FCDO's warning that it is illegal to photograph police or military personnel.

## Making a trip of it

Naadam fits naturally into a longer summer journey. The days around the festival are a good time to head into the countryside once the capital's celebrations are over, perhaps to the [Orkhon Valley](/posts/orkhon-valley-karakorum/) or a [family stay in a ger](/posts/staying-with-nomad-family-ger/). If you are building a two-week trip, our [14-day itinerary](/posts/mongolia-14-day-itinerary/) shows how the pieces can fit together.

Whatever you choose, give yourself time to sit on the grass and watch. Naadam is not a show put on for visitors. It is a country celebrating itself, and the best seat is often the one next to the families who came to cheer.
