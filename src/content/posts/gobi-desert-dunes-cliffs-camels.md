You'll start the climb as the sun drops. The sand is cool on the shaded side of the ridge and still warm on the other, and every step slides half a step back. Halfway up, your legs are burning and you stop to look behind you: the vehicle you arrived in is a toy, the camp a scatter of white dots, and beyond them the plain runs flat and green-gold to a line of dark mountains. At the crest the wind picks up. Sit down, push your hands into the sand and let it pour away down the slip face, and you may hear it, a low hum rising from the dune itself. Then the light turns the whole ridge the colour of apricots, and for a few minutes nobody says much at all.

The Gobi is the part of Mongolia that most people picture before they come, and it rarely disappoints. It is also often misunderstood. Much of it is not sand at all but gravel plain, scrub and low mountains, home to herding families and their animals. This guide covers the classic sights of the southern Gobi, when to go, and how to travel there well.

## A desert that is mostly not sand

The classic Gobi trip heads for the south, to the province of Ömnögovi and the Gobi Gurvansaikhan National Park, named after the Gurvan Saikhan mountains, the "Three Beauties". Mongolia's [official tourism website](https://www.gomongolia.gov.mn/explore-experience/) describes the Gobi as "far more than just barren land": a rich ecosystem of towering sand dunes, dramatic canyons, rare wildlife and the fossils of dinosaurs.

So the Gobi you will see is a landscape of variety: wide gravel plains where you can watch a vehicle's dust trail for an hour, rocky ranges, dry riverbeds, saxaul scrub, the occasional spring with a ring of green, and then, suddenly, a line of dunes. That variety is what makes a few days here so rewarding.

## Khongoryn Els: the singing dunes

The great dune field on the classic route is Khongoryn Els, inside the national park. The government's [tourism website](https://www.gomongolia.gov.mn/explore-experience/) calls it the Singing Sand Dunes, "where the sands sing with the wind", and on a breezy day you may hear a low hum as sand slides down the slopes. The dunes run in a long band at the foot of the mountains, and their shape and colour shift through the day as the wind and the light change.

Along the foot of the dunes you will often find patches of green pasture where herders' camels and horses graze. The contrast of grass and towering sand side by side is one of the most striking sights in the country.

**How to see it.** Most visitors stay at a camp near the dunes for a night or two. Climb in the late afternoon, when the sand has cooled and the low light shows every ripple. The climb is steep and soft, so take water, wear shoes you can empty easily, and give yourself time. If you are going up at sunset, carry a torch for the way down.

## Camels: the animal that makes the Gobi work

The two-humped Bactrian camel is part of daily life in the Gobi. Herding families keep them for transport, milk and wool, and you will see them grazing along the dune edges and around family camps. A short ride, or a longer camel trek, is a highlight of many trips. The pace is slow and rolling, and the high saddle between the humps gives you a wide view of the plain.

If you are visiting in late winter, Steppe & Sky's [festivals page](https://steppeandsky.tourground.com/festivals) describes the Thousand Camel Festival, usually held in early March in the Bulgan soum area of Ömnögovi, with camel races, camel polo and displays of camel-wool crafts. As with all festival dates in Mongolia, check the dates for your year before you plan around it.

**A note on riding.** Let the herder lead the camel and help you mount and dismount: the camel kneels, and the moment it stands up, back legs first, is a lurch worth being ready for. Lean back as it rises.

## The Flaming Cliffs at Bayanzag

North-east of the dunes lies Bayanzag, better known as the Flaming Cliffs. According to Mongolia's [official tourism website](https://www.gomongolia.gov.mn/explore-experience/), this is where dinosaur eggs were first discovered, in the 1920s, and it is still the best-known fossil site on a Gobi trip.

What you see today is a long escarpment of red and orange sandstone, carved into gullies and buttresses. At sunset the colour deepens until the whole cliff seems to glow, which is how the place earned its nickname. Walk along the rim, then drop down into the gullies to look back up at the formations.

**Leave everything where it is.** Anything that looks like bone or eggshell belongs where you found it. The FCDO [warns](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/entry-requirements), at the time of writing (November 2026), that Mongolia has strict rules on goods taken into or out of the country, and that you may be held in Mongolia if you become involved in a customs investigation. Take photographs, not souvenirs.

## Yolyn Am: ice in the desert

In the Gurvan Saikhan range is Yolyn Am, a deep, narrow gorge that Mongolia's [official tourism website](https://www.gomongolia.gov.mn/explore-experience/) calls Eagle Valley: "a breathtaking ice-filled canyon at the heart of the desert". Ice builds up in its shaded depths over winter.

The walk into the gorge follows a stream between steep rock walls, and it feels a world away from the plains outside. Look up for birds of prey on the cliffs, and look down for the pikas that dart between the rocks. How long the ice lasts into summer varies from year to year, so ask your guide what to expect; later in the season you may find only a trickle of water and cool shade.

## When to go

Steppe & Sky's [month-by-month guide](https://steppeandsky.tourground.com/when-to-go) singles out May and September as good months for the Gobi, before the summer heat and as the crowds fade, and lists the Gobi among the best regions in June too. High summer is visited as well, but expect hot days.

Whatever the month, the Gobi is where Mongolia's weather is at its most extreme. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) [says](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security), at the time of writing (November 2026), that the most extreme weather occurs in and around the Gobi Desert and the steppe, that temperatures in the country range from around +35°C in summer to -40°C in winter and at night, and that weather warnings for dust storms, strong winds and heatwaves are issued all year. Plan for hot sun, cold nights and sudden wind, and pack accordingly. Our [packing guide](/posts/what-to-pack-mongolia-weather/) covers the essentials.

## Getting there and getting around

The southern Gobi is a long way from Ulaanbaatar, and most journeys are by road with a driver and guide, or by a domestic flight to the provincial capital followed by road. The FCDO [notes](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) that outside the capital only about 10% of roads are tarmacked and signposting is minimal. Desert tracks branch and rejoin, and local knowledge matters.

A few other points from the FCDO are worth knowing, as of November 2026:

- **Prepare for remoteness.** Carry plenty of water and provisions, take backup communications, and share your route and timings. Large rural areas have no phone coverage.
- **Fuel.** The FCDO's [warnings page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/warnings-and-insurance) reports fuel shortages, including aviation fuel, that may affect flights, road travel and access to remote areas. Allow extra time.
- **Border areas.** The Gobi runs up to the border with China. The FCDO's [entry requirements page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/entry-requirements) explains that the authorities can treat zones of up to 100km inside the border as a border area, and that foreigners need permission from the State Frontier Guard Authority to travel there. The classic sights above are well-visited, but if you plan anything off the usual route, check with your operator.
- **Medical care.** The FCDO's [health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/health) warns that healthcare outside the capital is limited and it may be a long journey to the nearest facility, so make sure your insurance covers medical evacuation.

## How long to give it

The Gobi is not a place to rush. With the long drives from the capital, the classic loop of Yolyn Am, Khongoryn Els and the Flaming Cliffs takes several days, and more if you want time on a camel or a quiet day at the dunes. Many travellers combine it with central Mongolia, heading north from the desert to the [Orkhon Valley and Karakorum](/posts/orkhon-valley-karakorum/). Our [14-day itinerary](/posts/mongolia-14-day-itinerary/) shows one way to link the two.

## Travelling lightly

The Gobi looks empty, but it is home to families whose livelihoods depend on its thin grazing and scarce water. Stay on existing tracks where you can, take all rubbish away with you, use water carefully at camps, and ask before photographing people or their homes. A night at a herding family's camp, if your operator can arrange it, is one of the best ways to understand how people live here. Our guide to [staying with a nomad family](/posts/staying-with-nomad-family-ger/) explains what to expect.

And when you find yourself at the top of a dune at dusk, with the sand humming under you and the plain turning blue below, stay a little longer than you planned. That is what you came for.
