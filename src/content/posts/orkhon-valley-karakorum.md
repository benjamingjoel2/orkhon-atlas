You see the wall before anything else. It runs long and low across the grass, white and even, broken at intervals by stupas that catch the afternoon sun one after another like beads on a string. Behind it rise temple roofs with upturned eaves, and behind those the land simply keeps going: pasture, a river line of willows, hills turning blue in the distance. A herder rides past on the track outside the wall, not looking up. You are standing beside the site of the Mongol Empire's capital, and the most striking thing about it is how quiet it is.

The Orkhon Valley rewards people who like their history layered. Turkic khans, Uighur kings, the Mongol Empire and the first Buddhist monastery in the country all left their mark along the same river, and herders still move their animals across the same grassland. It is not a place of grand ruins in the way Rome or Angkor are. It is a place where you have to slow down and let a landscape explain itself.

## Why UNESCO lists a whole valley

The [Orkhon Valley Cultural Landscape](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1081/) was inscribed on the World Heritage List on 7 July 2004. What is unusual is that the listing protects a landscape, not just a set of monuments. UNESCO's brief description says the 121,967-hectare site covers an extensive area of pastureland on both banks of the Orkhon River, with archaeological remains dating back to the 6th century, and that "the grassland is still grazed by Mongolian nomadic pastoralists".

The reasoning behind the listing is worth knowing before you go, because it changes how you look at the place. The World Heritage Committee's decision, quoted in the [nomination file](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1081rev.pdf), says that underpinning everything in the valley for the past two millennia "has been a strong culture of nomadic pastoralism", a culture it describes as still central to Mongolian society. The cities came and went. The herding stayed.

So the gers you pass on the drive in, the horses grazing near the monastery wall, the families moving between summer and autumn pastures: these are not a backdrop to the heritage site. They are part of it.

## Karakorum: a capital you have to imagine

The Mongol capital of Karakorum sat here, by the modern town of Kharkhorin. The nomination file sets out its story: Chinggis Khaan decided to establish a city at this location in 1220, and construction was completed in 1235 under his son Ögedei, when Karakorum was declared capital of the Mongol Empire. It became the empire's administrative, trade and cultural centre and a meeting point between East and West, receiving delegations from across Asia and Europe.

Historical documents describe one of its wonders, a silver tree-fountain made by the French artisan Guillaume Boucher for the Great Khan, with four sculpted lions from whose mouths drinks poured during festivities. It is one of those details that makes a lost city feel suddenly real.

Very little of Karakorum stands above ground today. The nomination file records excavations by Mongolian, Russian, Japanese and German teams over the past century. Walking the site, you need to bring your imagination: low earthworks, scattered stone, the outline of a plan in the grass. It helps enormously to go with a guide who can tell you what you are looking at, and to visit the monastery next door, which was built partly to keep the memory of the capital alive.

## Erdene Zuu: the monastery on the ruins

[Erdene Zuu](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1081rev.pdf) is the reason most people come to Kharkhorin, and the wall you saw first belongs to it. According to the nomination file, it was the first Buddhist monastery to be established in Mongolia, founded on the territory of the old imperial capital in 1586 by Avtai Sain Khan, with the aim of spreading Buddhism through the country. Part of its prestige came precisely from that location on the ruins of Karakorum.

The monastery is surrounded by 108 stupas joined by a stone wall. Inside, the nomination file explains, the space was arranged according to the traditional layout of a Mongolian camp, known as the khüree. It is a building designed by and for a nomadic people, and once you know that, the open spaces between the temples make more sense.

Much of Erdene Zuu was destroyed during the religious purges of the 1930s. Many thousands of religious objects were preserved, though, and the principal temples still stand, including the three Zuu temples, the Laviran temple, the Janraiseg temple and the Golden Stupa.

This is a religious site as well as a museum. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) asks visitors, at the time of writing (November 2026), to show respect in Buddhist monasteries, always ask permission before taking photographs, and not to touch sacred images or objects. Walk clockwise, keep your voice low when monks are at prayer, and dress modestly.

## Beyond Kharkhorin: Turkic stones, a Uighur city and a mountain hermitage

If you have time, the wider valley is where the visit deepens. The nomination file names several protected sites; three of them are worth seeking out with a driver who knows the tracks.

**The Turkic memorials of Khöshöö Tsaidam.** In the Tsaidam Lake valley, along the western part of the Orkhon River, stand two large memorial complexes to Bilge Khan (683–734) and his younger brother Kul Tegin (684–731), commander-in-chief of the Turkic armies. The nomination file calls them the most important known archaeological remains of the Turkic Empire, and the long runic inscriptions on their steles have been a key source for the history of Central Asia. They were first brought to scholars' attention in 1889.

**Khar Balgas.** This was the capital of the Uighur Empire in the 8th and 9th centuries. The nomination file describes a city stretching over some 50 square kilometres, with a fortified wall, a royal palace, barracks, shops, temples and craftsmen's districts; the palace walls alone measured 412 by 491 metres. Today it is earthworks and broken walls on the open plain, eerie and enormous.

**Tövkhön monastery.** This hermitage sits at the top of Tövkhön Khan mountain, which the nomination file gives as 2,268 metres. It was established as a retreat for meditation and art, and included the studio of Öndör Gegeen Zanabazar, the Buddhist leader and artist whose cast Buddha statues are admired worldwide and who created the Soyombo script. The site was chosen beside two caves used for secluded meditation. Getting there involves a rough drive and a climb, and the reward is a sense of why someone would choose this spot to think.

The distances between these sites are real and the tracks are rough. Treat the wider valley as a day or more, not an add-on to an afternoon at Erdene Zuu.

## When to go

Summer is when the valley is green, the river is full and herding families are on their summer pastures. Weather can still turn quickly. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) notes, at the time of writing, that conditions in Mongolia range from +35°C in summer to −40°C in winter and at night, that weather often changes without warning, and that warnings are issued all year for flooding, dust storms, strong winds and heatwaves. Pack layers and rain gear whatever the month.

Ticks are worth a thought if you plan to walk or ride through long grass and woodland. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/country/151/mongolia) says ticks are most active from early spring to late autumn, and that tick-borne encephalitis cases have been reported in several provinces. Check its current list against your route, use repellent, cover up and check yourself each evening.

## Getting there and getting around

The Orkhon Valley lies in central Mongolia and is usually reached overland from Ulaanbaatar. Roads beyond the capital deserve respect. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) says only about 10% of roads outside Ulaanbaatar are tarmacked and signposting is minimal, and it lists low driving standards, speeding, poorly maintained vehicles and difficult terrain among the causes of a high number of road accidents. Its advice for rural travel is to research the route, ask local drivers about conditions, carry back-up communications, pack water and provisions, and share your plans. In August 2026 it also added a warning about reported fuel shortages that may affect road travel and access to remote areas.

In practice, most visitors travel with an experienced driver and guide, which also solves the problem of finding the more remote sites. Health care is limited outside the capital: the [FCDO's health advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/health) recommends insurance that covers medical evacuation, and notes that hospitals ask for 80% of treatment costs in advance.

## Ways to experience the valley

**By vehicle, with time to stop.** The classic way is overland, staying in ger camps or with families, and pairing Kharkhorin with the river and the hermitage. It also links easily with a southern loop through [the Gobi](/posts/gobi-desert-dunes-cliffs-camels/), as in our [14-day itinerary](/posts/mongolia-14-day-itinerary/).

**On horseback.** The open grassland along the river is natural riding country. If you have never ridden before, read our [beginner's guide to horse trekking](/posts/horse-trekking-mongolia-beginners/) first.

**With a family.** Because the valley's heritage is its herding life, a night with a family here is not a detour from the history but part of it. Our guide to [staying with a nomad family in a ger](/posts/staying-with-nomad-family-ger/) explains how to do it well.

## Before you go

- Allow at least a full day for Kharkhorin and Erdene Zuu, and more if you want to reach the Turkic memorials, Khar Balgas or Tövkhön.
- Go with a guide; Karakorum in particular is hard to read on your own.
- Ask before every photograph inside the monastery, and do not touch sacred objects.
- Check the [FCDO's latest advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia) for fuel, weather and road conditions shortly before you travel.
- Carry your passport at all times; the FCDO says police will not accept a copy.

Stand by the monastery wall at the end of the day, when the tour vehicles have gone and the light goes gold across the grass, and the valley's long story feels less like a list of dates and more like one continuous life lived in the same place.
