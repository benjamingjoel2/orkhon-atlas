It starts as a perfect morning. You step out of the ger into thin, cold air with frost on the grass and the sun just clearing the hills, and you are glad of your hat and gloves. By mid-morning you have peeled off two layers. By lunchtime you are hunting for sunscreen and wishing you had brought a wider hat. At four o'clock a wall of dark cloud rolls over the ridge, the wind picks up, and for ten minutes it hails. Then the sky clears, the light turns gold, and by the time the stars come out you are back in every layer you own, sitting close to the stove.

That is a normal summer day on the Mongolian steppe, and it is the single most useful thing to understand before you pack. You are not packing for a climate; you are packing for weather, and a lot of it. This guide explains how to do that without hauling a wardrobe across the country.

## Understanding the weather

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) puts it plainly. Its [safety and security page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) describes Mongolia as a huge country with conditions ranging from +35°C in summer to -40°C in winter and at night, and says weather conditions often change without warning. It notes frequent weather warnings all year round for flooding, dust storms, heavy snow and blizzards, strong winds and heatwaves, and says the most extreme conditions occur in and around the Gobi Desert and the steppe.

For a sense of the seasons, Steppe & Sky's [month-by-month guide](https://steppeandsky.tourground.com/when-to-go) gives indicative figures for the central steppe around Ulaanbaatar, and is careful to call them a guide rather than a forecast. July, the warmest month, is shown at around 12°C at night and 28°C by day; September at around 2°C at night and 19°C by day; and January at around -30°C at night and -14°C by day. It notes that the Gobi runs warmer by day and the mountains and north colder. The big lesson is the gap between day and night, which in Steppe & Sky's figures is around fifteen degrees or more in most months.

## The golden rule: pack light and soft

Most trips outside Ulaanbaatar travel by four-wheel drive, and on some routes your luggage goes on a horse or camel. Steppe & Sky's [packing page](https://steppeandsky.tourground.com/what-to-pack) recommends one soft duffel of up to 15kg and a daypack, and that is a good discipline whoever you travel with. Hard suitcases are awkward to load and do not survive the tracks well.

Inside the duffel, use packing cubes or dry bags. Dust gets into everything, and rain sometimes does too. Keep a few zip-lock bags for electronics, documents and anything you want to keep clean.

## Layers, not bulk

A single good layering system will see you through almost any summer day:

- **Base layers.** Quick-drying tops and leggings in merino or synthetic fabric. Two or three sets.
- **Mid layer.** A light fleece or wool jumper.
- **Insulation.** A packable synthetic or down jacket. You will wear it most evenings, even in July.
- **Shell.** A properly waterproof and windproof jacket, plus waterproof over-trousers. The wind is constant on the open steppe, and showers come suddenly.
- **Trousers.** Comfortable walking trousers; avoid jeans, which are cold when wet and slow to dry.
- **Warm hat and light gloves.** Yes, in summer too.
- **Something modest and smarter.** For city evenings and for visiting monasteries, where covered shoulders and knees are respectful.

For winter trips, Steppe & Sky's list adds a heavy down parka, insulated trousers and mittens, and insulated boots rated well below freezing. Winter travel in Mongolia is a serious undertaking, and an operator who runs winter trips should give you a specific kit list.

## Footwear

You will walk on rock, sand, wet grass and, depending on the season, frozen ground. Bring:

- **Walking boots** with ankle support, broken in before you leave.
- **Light trainers or camp shoes** for evenings.
- **Sandals** for showers and river crossings.
- **Slip-on shoes** for getting in and out of the ger at night without lacing up.
- **Gaiters** if you are heading to the dunes or walking through wet grass.
- **Good socks**, several pairs of wool or synthetic.

If you are riding, add boots with a small heel; see our guide to [horse trekking for beginners](/posts/horse-trekking-mongolia-beginners/) for the full riding kit.

## Sleeping warm

Gers are cosy when the stove is lit, but stoves burn out in the small hours and nights can be cold even in summer. Ask your operator what bedding is provided. Many camps supply blankets; wild camping and some family stays call for your own sleeping bag. A three-season bag and a liner cover most summer trips. A warm hat and thermal leggings to sleep in are worth their weight, and earplugs and an eye mask help when the sun rises very early.

## Sun, wind and dust

The steppe sun is fierce: there is little shade for hours at a time. [TravelHealthPro's sun protection advice](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/sun-protection) recommends sunscreen with an SPF of 30 or above and a 4 or 5-star UVA rating, together with shade, clothing, hats and sunglasses. It notes that the sun's rays are strongest at high altitude, and that snow increases exposure by reflecting UV, which is worth remembering on a winter trip.

Pack:

- High-factor sunscreen and lip balm with SPF
- A wide-brimmed hat with a chin strap, because the wind will take it otherwise
- Wraparound sunglasses
- A buff or neck gaiter to pull over your face in dust
- Rich moisturiser and eye drops; the air is dry

## Health and first aid

Pharmacies are scarce outside the towns, and the [FCDO's health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/health) notes that medical products in pharmacies are mainly from China and Russia, so you may not easily recognise what you need. It advises bringing prescription medication and a first aid kit with you. At the time of writing (November 2026), it also warns that healthcare outside Ulaanbaatar is limited and recommends insurance covering medical treatment and air ambulance evacuation.

A sensible kit includes:

- All your prescription medicines, plus spares, in their original packaging
- Pain relief, rehydration sachets and anti-diarrhoea tablets
- Blister plasters, antiseptic wipes and hand sanitiser
- Travel-sickness remedies for long drives on rough tracks
- Insect repellent, especially for the north in summer
- Toilet paper and a small trowel for days away from facilities

Three health notes from official sources are worth acting on before you pack:

- **Ticks.** [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/countries/mongolia) says there is a risk of tick-borne encephalitis in some areas, with ticks most active from early spring to late autumn. Long trousers, repellent and a daily tick check are sensible.
- **Air quality.** The FCDO says air pollution in Ulaanbaatar, especially in winter, may aggravate bronchial, sinus or asthma conditions. If that applies to you, bring your inhalers and consider a well-fitting mask.
- **Altitude.** TravelHealthPro notes points above 2,500 metres in Mongolia, where altitude illness is a risk. That mostly affects high-mountain trips in the west.

Check vaccine recommendations at least eight weeks before you travel, as the FCDO advises.

## Power and electronics

Power in camps is often solar or generator-based, available only at certain times and shared between guests. Bring a head torch with spare batteries, a large power bank, a multi-port USB charger and a universal adaptor. A car charger is useful on long drives. Spare camera batteries drain fast in the cold, so keep them in an inside pocket.

The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) notes that there are large rural areas with no network coverage. Download offline maps, music and reading before you leave the city.

## Documents and money

- **Passport** with at least six months' validity after arrival. At the time of writing, the [FCDO's entry requirements page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/entry-requirements) gives that as the rule for British citizens, and says they can visit for tourism for up to 30 days without a visa until 31 December 2026. Check the rules for your own passport.
- **Carry the original.** The FCDO says police will not accept a copy and you could be fined. Keep photocopies of the photo page and entry stamp separately.
- **Insurance details**, including the emergency number. The FCDO says [insurance should cover your itinerary and planned activities](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/warnings-and-insurance).
- **Cash.** Cards work in much of Ulaanbaatar, but cash in local currency is more useful in the countryside. If you are carrying a lot of cash in, the FCDO says you must declare amounts of 10,000 US dollars or more.
- **A waterproof pouch** to keep it all dry and dust-free.

## Season by season, in brief

- **June to August:** the layering system above, a waterproof shell, sun kit and insect repellent. Expect heat in the Gobi and cold nights everywhere.
- **September:** the same, with a warmer jacket and hat; nights get cold quickly.
- **October:** treat it as early winter, especially in the west. Our guide to the [Golden Eagle Festival](/posts/golden-eagle-festival-altai/) covers dressing for a day standing on frozen steppe.
- **November to March:** specialist cold-weather clothing and boots, and an operator experienced in winter travel.

## A final word

Leave some room in your bag. You may want to bring home felt slippers, a cashmere scarf or a sack of dried curd from a family you stayed with. And you will almost certainly want that extra layer at some point.
