The first thing you notice is how small the horse is. You had pictured something tall and glossy; instead you are standing next to a stocky, shaggy-maned animal that comes up to about your shoulder and regards you with complete indifference. Then you are in the saddle, the wrangler gives a short whistle, and the herd moves off as one across a meadow full of wild flowers. The horse knows exactly where it is going. Within the hour you have stopped gripping the reins quite so hard, and somewhere around the second river crossing you notice that you are grinning.

Riding in Mongolia is one of the great travel experiences, and you do not need to be an accomplished rider to do it. You do need the right trip, the right expectations and the right kit. This guide is written for people who have ridden a little, or not at all, and want to know what they are letting themselves in for.

## Why Mongolia is different

Horses are not a tourist attraction here; they are part of daily life. [Wikipedia's entry on the Mongolian horse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolian_horse) notes that the breed is the country's native horse, that herders still keep more than three million of them, outnumbering the human population, and that they live outdoors all year in temperatures from around 30°C in summer to -40°C in winter, grazing and finding food on their own. They are used for herders' daily work, for transport and for racing, and mare's milk is turned into *airag*, the national drink you are likely to be offered in a ger.

All of that shapes the riding. Mongolian horses are hardy, sure-footed and independent. They are generally not the kind of horse that has spent its life in a riding school; many are semi-wild herd animals that are caught and saddled for the trek. They tend to be calm in a group and keen to keep up with their companions, and they can be quick to react to something new. The wranglers who look after them know each animal's character, which is why a good wrangler matters more than almost anything else on a trek.

## Choosing a trek as a beginner

There is a wide range of trips, from a few hours' ride out from a ger camp to multi-week expeditions. For a first trip, think about these questions:

**How many hours a day?** Look for a trip with shorter riding days at the start and a rest day or two, and ask your operator directly how long you will be in the saddle. Even fit people find the first days hard on the knees, hips and backside.

**What is the support?** Most treks either use a support vehicle that carries luggage and camping gear to the next camp, or pack horses. A vehicle offers a useful escape route if you are tired or unwell. Ask about it.

**What is the grading?** Operators grade their trips, and it is worth taking those grades seriously. Steppe & Sky's [list of adventures](https://steppeandsky.tourground.com/adventures), for example, grades its 10-day Orkhon Valley horse trek as *Challenging*, while gentler trips with only short rides carry an *Easy* grade. If you are a novice, either pick something with less riding, or talk honestly to the operator about whether you can build up during the trip.

**Where?** The Orkhon Valley in central Mongolia is a classic first riding destination: river meadows, forested slopes and the [Orkhon Valley Cultural Landscape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orkhon_Valley), a UNESCO World Heritage Site recognised for representing more than two millennia of nomadic pastoral tradition. Riding through a landscape inscribed precisely for its herding culture, past herders doing the same, is a special thing. See our guide to [the Orkhon Valley and Karakorum](/posts/orkhon-valley-karakorum/) for what else is there.

## A day on the trail

Days fall into a gentle rhythm. Breakfast at camp while the wranglers catch and saddle the horses. A briefing from your guide on the route, the river crossings and anything to watch for. Then a morning ride, usually at a walk with some trotting and, for those who want it, the occasional canter on open ground. Lunch is a picnic or a stop at a family ger. The afternoon ride brings you to the next camp, where the horses are hobbled or turned out to graze and you are left to stretch, wash in the river, and watch the sky go pink.

You will spend a lot of time at a walk, and that is part of the pleasure. The steppe is quiet, and horses move at a pace that lets you look around properly: at marmots standing guard by their burrows, at a family moving their herd, at the way cloud shadows race across the hills.

## Basic horse sense

Your wrangler and guide will give you a proper briefing, and you should follow it over anything you read here. Some general principles help:

- **Tell the truth about your experience.** Wranglers match horses to riders. If you overstate your ability, you may get a horse that is too lively for you.
- **Approach calmly.** Move slowly around horses, talk to them, and let the wrangler show you how to approach and mount.
- **Ride with the group.** Horses here are herd animals and are happiest together. Do not race off ahead or fall a long way behind without telling your guide.
- **Speak up early.** If you are sore, nervous or want to slow down, say so. A good guide would rather adjust the pace than see you struggle in silence.
- **Hold on to your reins in camera moments.** Photographs are best taken when the horse is standing still, or when someone else is holding it.

## What to wear and bring

The right kit makes an enormous difference. Steppe & Sky's [packing list](https://steppeandsky.tourground.com/what-to-pack) includes a riding section that is a sensible starting point: a well-fitting riding helmet, padded or seamless riding tights or trousers, half chaps or riding gaiters, boots with a small heel that will not slip through stirrups, thin riding gloves, a small saddle bag or bum bag for water and snacks, anti-chafe balm, and a light waterproof you can ride in. It notes that some trips provide riding helmets on the ground, so check with your operator; if you have a helmet you know fits, bring it.

Beyond that:

- **Layers.** Mornings can be cold and afternoons hot. A thin base layer, a fleece and a windproof jacket cover most days.
- **Sun protection.** Wide-brimmed hat with a chin strap, sunglasses, high-factor sunscreen and lip balm.
- **Sleeping bag.** Nights can be cold even in summer; ask your operator what rating you need.
- **Soft luggage.** Bags travel in a vehicle or on a pack horse, so a soft duffel is better than a hard case.

Our full guide to [what to pack for Mongolia's weather](/posts/what-to-pack-mongolia-weather/) has more detail.

## Getting fit beforehand

You do not need to be an athlete, but a few rides at a local stables before you go will help enormously, even if they are short. They will get your muscles used to the saddle and remind you how to mount, stop and steer. Add some walking, cycling or squats to strengthen your legs and core. People who arrive having ridden a few times in the preceding month usually enjoy the first days much more.

## Health, safety and insurance

Riding in remote country carries real risks, and it is worth being clear-eyed about them.

**Insurance.** The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) says your [travel insurance should cover your itinerary, planned activities and expenses in an emergency](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/warnings-and-insurance). Check, at the time of writing (November 2026) and again when you buy a policy, that horse riding is explicitly covered, and that the policy includes medical evacuation. The [FCDO's health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/health) says healthcare outside Ulaanbaatar is limited, it may be a long journey to the nearest medical facility, and hospitals may ask you to pay 80% of treatment costs in advance. It specifically recommends cover for an air ambulance.

**Remoteness.** The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) notes that large rural areas have no phone coverage. A reputable operator will carry backup communications such as a satellite phone; ask what they use.

**Ticks.** [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/countries/mongolia) says there is a risk of tick-borne encephalitis in some areas of Mongolia, with ticks most active from early spring to late autumn. Riding through long grass and woodland is exactly the kind of activity that brings you into contact with ticks. Wear long trousers, use repellent, check yourself each evening, and speak to a travel health professional about whether vaccination is right for you.

**Animals and rabies.** TravelHealthPro says rabies is a risk in Mongolia and advises travellers to avoid contact with all animals. The [rabies factsheet](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/rabies) explains that most human cases follow a bite or scratch from an infected dog. You will be handling horses, of course, but steer clear of camp and herding dogs, and if you are bitten or scratched by any animal, wash the wound thoroughly and seek medical advice urgently.

**Vaccines.** The FCDO recommends checking the latest vaccine advice at least eight weeks before you travel.

## When to ride

Steppe & Sky's [month-by-month guide](https://steppeandsky.tourground.com/when-to-go) describes June to September as the main season, and singles out August, which it notes is often the wettest month, as excellent for riding, with lush pastures. June is green with fewer visitors, July the warmest and busiest, and September crisp and clear. Expect some rain on most treks, and pack accordingly.

## A gentle way in

If a full trek feels like too much, start smaller. Many itineraries include a few hours of riding from a ger camp or a host family, which is enough to find out whether you love it. Our [14-day itinerary](/posts/mongolia-14-day-itinerary/) leaves room for a short ride in the Orkhon Valley, and a [stay with a nomad family](/posts/staying-with-nomad-family-ger/) often comes with a chance to ride out with the herd.

Then, if you find yourself thinking about that shaggy little horse long after you get home, you will know it is time to plan the longer trek.
