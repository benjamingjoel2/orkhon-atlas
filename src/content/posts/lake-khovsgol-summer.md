The water is so clear it takes a moment to understand it. You crouch on a shingle beach, put your hand in, and the stones on the bottom look close enough to touch long after your arm says otherwise. It is cold, properly cold, even under a high summer sun. Behind you the larch forest climbs the hillside in dark green ranks; in front of you the lake runs north until it meets the sky. A yak grazes along the shore with the unhurried air of an animal that owns the place. Somewhere a horse snorts. That is all you hear.

After the open steppe and the Gobi, Lake Khövsgöl feels like another country. It is mountain and forest, cold water and wildflowers, and it rewards travellers who give it time rather than ticking it off.

## A lake unlike anywhere else in Mongolia

UNESCO's [biosphere reserve profile](https://www.unesco.org/en/mab/khuvsgul-lake) describes Khövsgöl as "Mongolia's deepest and clearest lake, which holds the majority of Mongolia's freshwater". It has existed in its present form for seven to eight million years. Ninety-six rivers and streams flow into it, and only one river, the Eg, flows out, so its water is exchanged very slowly. That slow turnover is part of why the lake is so clear, and part of why it needs looking after.

In June 2022, UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere Programme added Khuvsgul Lake to its World Network of Biosphere Reserves, at the session of its International Co-ordinating Council held in Paris from 13 to 17 June, according to [UNESCO's announcement](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-designates-11-new-biosphere-reserves). The reserve covers more than 1.5 million hectares of northern Mongolia, and its landscapes range, in UNESCO's words, from floodplain meadows and dense taiga to forest-steppe, with surrounding habitats running from tundra to forest-steppe.

Biosphere reserves are not wilderness parks with the people taken out. They are places where conservation and everyday life are meant to work together, and that is the best way to understand Khövsgöl: a protected landscape that is also home to herding communities.

## Who lives here

About 7,000 people live within the reserve, according to [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-designates-11-new-biosphere-reserves). The biosphere profile names Tsaatan, Darkhad, Uriankhai, Khalkh and Buryat communities. Most people live from livestock, seasonal haymaking and small vegetable plots, and in autumn locals and people from nearby districts gather nuts and fruit. Tourism is growing, mainly horse riding, hiking and boat trips.

Tsaatan families herd reindeer in the taiga. For many visitors, meeting them is the reason for coming this far north, and it is the part of a Khövsgöl trip that most needs care. These are families, not an attraction. Visits should be arranged in advance, with families who want visitors and on terms agreed beforehand. Steppe & Sky's [responsible travel guidelines](https://steppeandsky.tourground.com/responsible-travel) put it simply: guests, not spectators; no staged performances; no turning up unannounced; and herding life comes first. Ask before you photograph anyone, keep groups small, and let the family's routine set the pace.

If you are spending a night with any herding family in the region, our guide to [staying with a nomad family in a ger](/posts/staying-with-nomad-family-ger/) covers the etiquette in more detail.

## What a summer day can look like

There is no single way to spend time at Khövsgöl, and the best trips leave room to do very little.

**Walking the shore and the hills.** Hiking is one of the main activities UNESCO lists for the area. Paths and tracks follow the shoreline and climb into the forest above it. Wildflowers fill the meadows in summer, and from any high point the lake opens out below you in shades of blue that do not look real.

**Riding.** Horse riding is the other classic. A day ride along the shore is a gentle introduction; longer rides into the hills or towards the taiga are for confident riders with good kit. If you are new to it, read our [beginner's guide to horse trekking](/posts/horse-trekking-mongolia-beginners/) first.

**On the water.** Boat trips are part of the local tourism scene. The lake is very cold, so treat any time on the water with respect: wear a life jacket and go with someone who knows the conditions.

**Watching wildlife.** The biosphere profile lists nine fish species including lenok, Mongolian grayling and burbot, and mammals including snow leopard, red deer, reindeer and Siberian ibex. You are far more likely to see birds than big mammals: white-tailed eagle, osprey and whooper swan are among the species it names. Bring binoculars. If you want to fish, ask locally about current rules and permits before you cast a line.

**Doing nothing at all.** Sit on the shore at the end of the day, when the light goes long and gold and the forest turns black against it. Northern summer evenings stretch on, and there is no better way to spend one.

## Weather: pack for every season

Khövsgöl sits in the mountains of the far north, and its summer is short and changeable. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) says, at the time of writing (November 2026), that conditions in Mongolia range from +35°C in summer to −40°C in winter and at night, that weather often changes without warning, and that warnings are issued throughout the year for flooding, strong winds, heavy snow and other hazards.

In practice, that means packing as if you will see several seasons in a day, because you may. A warm layer, a waterproof jacket, a hat and gloves belong in your bag even in July. Nights in a ger or tent can be cold. Our [packing guide](/posts/what-to-pack-mongolia-weather/) has a full list.

Ticks are worth a thought in forest and long grass. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/country/151/mongolia) notes that ticks are most active from early spring to late autumn and that tick-borne encephalitis has been reported in parts of Mongolia. Check its current list of affected areas against your route, use repellent, tuck trousers into socks on forest walks and check yourself every evening. It also advises avoiding contact with all animals because of the rabies risk, which applies to dogs around camps and settlements as much as anywhere.

## Getting there, and the border question

Khövsgöl is a long way from Ulaanbaatar, and the journey is part of the trip. Most routes head north to the provincial capital, Mörön, and on to the lake's southern shore. However you travel, allow plenty of time.

Roads outside the capital need patience and a good driver. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) says only about 10% of roads outside Ulaanbaatar are tarmacked, signposting is minimal, and large rural areas have no phone network. Its [warnings page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/warnings-and-insurance) added a note in August 2026 about reported fuel shortages, including aviation fuel, which may affect domestic flights, road travel and access to remote areas. Check with your operator or airline close to departure and build slack into your plans.

The lake's northern reaches are not far from the Russian border, and that matters. According to the [FCDO's entry requirements page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/entry-requirements), foreign nationals are not routinely allowed into border areas, the authorities can treat zones up to 100 km inside the border as border areas, and you need permission from the State Frontier Guard Authority to travel in them. If your plans include the far north of the lake or the taiga beyond, ask your operator whether any part of your route needs a permit, and leave time to arrange it.

The same page reminds visitors that hotels register guests through an app, but independent travellers must register themselves with the Mongolian Immigration Agency. Carry your passport at all times: the [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) says police will not accept a copy.

## Health and safety when you are far from help

Khövsgöl is remote, and help can be a long way off. The [FCDO's health advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/health) says healthcare outside Ulaanbaatar is limited, doctors may not speak English, and hospitals ask for 80% of treatment costs to be paid in advance. Take a first aid kit and any prescription medication with you, and make sure your insurance covers air ambulance evacuation. Tell someone your route and when you expect to be back in signal.

## Treading lightly

A lake that exchanges its water this slowly does not forgive pollution easily. Simple habits make a difference:

- Take all your rubbish away with you, including food waste, batteries and wet wipes.
- Do not wash with soap or detergent in the lake or the streams that feed it.
- Stick to existing tracks with vehicles; meadows and shorelines recover slowly.
- Use a stove rather than a fire where wood is scarce, and never leave a fire unattended.
- Keep your distance from wildlife and nesting birds, and never fly a drone near herds, homes or animals without permission.
- Buy crafts and food directly from local families where you can, so more of what you spend stays here.

## Fitting Khövsgöl into a trip

Because of the distances, Khövsgöl works best as the focus of a trip or as a northern loop of a longer journey, rather than a quick add-on. It pairs well with central Mongolia: [the Orkhon Valley and Karakorum](/posts/orkhon-valley-karakorum/) lie in the same broad direction from the capital. If this is your first visit to Mongolia, our [first-timer's guide](/posts/first-timers-guide-mongolia/) and [14-day itinerary](/posts/mongolia-14-day-itinerary/) will help you decide whether to head north, south to [the Gobi](/posts/gobi-desert-dunes-cliffs-camels/), or both.

Whichever way you plan it, keep one evening free. Find a quiet stretch of shore, put your hand in that impossible water once more, and watch the light leave the hills.
