You'll notice the sky first. Somewhere on the drive out of Ulaanbaatar the apartment blocks thin out, the traffic loosens its grip, and the road starts to rise and fall over grassland that runs all the way to the horizon. The light is enormous. A white ger sits on a slope with a motorbike parked beside it and a thread of smoke leaving the stovepipe. Horses graze without a fence in sight. If you open the window, the air smells of grass, dust and sun-warmed earth.

That first afternoon tends to rearrange your idea of distance. Mongolia is a country where a "short drive" can fill a morning, where the road becomes a set of tracks fanning across the steppe, and where the people you meet along the way may move their homes with the seasons. It is also a country that rewards a little preparation. This guide covers the things first-time visitors most often ask about: when to go, what paperwork you need, how to stay healthy, how to get around and how to choose where to spend your days.

## When to go

Most travellers come in summer. Steppe & Sky's [month-by-month guide](https://steppeandsky.tourground.com/when-to-go) describes roughly June to September as the main season, with long days and green steppe, and calls July the warmest and busiest month, when Naadam fills the calendar. August is often the wettest month; September brings crisp, clear days and golden larch.

Mongolia's climate is continental and it can be extreme. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) [notes](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) that temperatures range from around +35°C in summer to -40°C in winter and at night, and that "weather conditions often change without warning". It says weather warnings are issued all year round for flooding, dust storms, heavy snow and blizzards, strong winds and heatwaves, and that the most extreme conditions occur in and around the Gobi Desert and the steppe.

In practice that means even a July trip needs a warm layer for the evenings and a waterproof for the afternoon showers. Our [packing guide](/posts/what-to-pack-mongolia-weather/) goes through it piece by piece.

Winter is a different journey altogether: clear skies, frozen lakes, very few visitors and serious cold. It suits experienced travellers who are prepared for it, and the lunar new year, Tsagaan Sar, falls in this season.

## Paperwork: visas, passports and registration

Entry rules depend on your nationality, so start with your own government's advice and the [Immigration Agency of Mongolia](https://immigration.gov.mn/en/). The agency publishes a list of countries whose citizens are exempt from tourist visa requirements for visits of up to 30 days in 2026.

For British citizens, the FCDO's [entry requirements page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/entry-requirements) says, at the time of writing (November 2026), that:

- you can visit Mongolia for tourism for up to 30 days without a visa until 31 December 2026;
- your passport must have an expiry date at least six months after the date you arrive;
- you should make sure the border officer stamps your passport, or you could have problems when you leave;
- your hotel or landlord will register you using an app, but if you are travelling independently or staying with friends you must register yourself with the Mongolian Immigration Agency (you do not need to go in person);
- adults travelling with children other than their own should carry a notarised letter from the parents or legal guardian.

If your trip runs into 2027, check again before you book: the visa-free arrangement for British citizens is time-limited.

Once you are in the country, the FCDO [advises](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) that you always carry your passport. Police will not accept a copy and you could be fined. Keep copies of the photo page and the page with your entry stamp somewhere separate.

## Staying healthy

See a travel health professional well before you go. The FCDO's [health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/health) recommends checking the latest vaccine recommendations at least eight weeks before your trip. TravelHealthPro's [Mongolia page](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/country/151/mongolia) lists the vaccines it recommends for most travellers and those it recommends for some travellers depending on the trip, and it notes that altitude is a risk in parts of the country.

A few other points from the FCDO are worth knowing before you book, as of November 2026:

- **Healthcare is limited outside the capital.** If you are ill or injured in a remote area, it may be a long journey to the nearest medical facility, and doctors may not speak English.
- **You pay up front.** The FCDO says hospitals will ask you to pay 80% of the treatment cost in advance, and there is no reciprocal healthcare agreement between the UK and Mongolia.
- **Insurance matters.** Make sure your policy covers full medical expenses and an air ambulance home, as well as the activities you plan, such as riding.
- **Bring your own medicines.** Pharmacies mainly stock products from China and Russia, so bring prescription medication and a first aid kit.
- **Winter air.** Air pollution in Ulaanbaatar is high, especially in winter, and may aggravate asthma and other conditions.

In an emergency the numbers are 103 for an ambulance, 102 for police and 101 for fire, according to the FCDO's [getting help page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/getting-help).

## Ulaanbaatar: your first day or two

Almost every trip begins and ends in the capital, and it is worth giving it time. Ulaanbaatar is busy, young and full of contrasts: monasteries and museums a short walk from glass towers, and ger districts climbing the hills around the centre. A day or two here also helps you get over the flight before the long drives.

The FCDO's [safety advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) is practical and sensible:

- Pickpockets may operate in markets and crowded places, and the risk rises during Tsagaan Sar and Naadam.
- Keep your phone, passport and money in a secured bag on your person, and leave valuables in a hotel safe if you can.
- Avoid being out alone at night. Ask your hotel or restaurant to order a car, use licensed taxis, or use the trackable UBCab app.
- Driving in the city is hazardous because the roads are heavily congested.

## Getting around: the roads, the tracks and the time

Distances are the single biggest thing first-timers underestimate. The FCDO [says](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) that outside the capital only about 10% of the country's roads are tarmacked and signposting is minimal. It recommends researching your route, asking local guides or drivers about conditions ahead, carrying backup communications such as a satellite phone, packing plenty of water and provisions, and sharing your route and timings with someone.

There is also a current issue to watch. At the time of writing (November 2026), the FCDO's [warnings page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/warnings-and-insurance) reports fuel shortages, including aviation fuel, which may affect domestic and international flights, road travel and access to some remote areas. Check with your airline or transport provider and allow extra time.

All of this is why most visitors travel with a local driver and guide rather than driving themselves. If you do plan to drive, the FCDO says British visitors need a 1968 international driving permit along with a UK licence, and that you cannot buy one outside the UK.

Phone signal is another adjustment. Coverage is good in the cities, but the FCDO notes that large rural areas have no network at all. Many travellers find that a few days offline is part of the appeal; just make sure someone at home knows your route.

## Choosing your first region

Mongolia is vast, and the temptation is to see all of it. Most first trips are happier combining two areas and giving them time. Here is how the main regions feel:

- **The Gobi.** Dunes, red cliffs, canyons and camels in the south. Big skies and long drives. See [our Gobi guide](/posts/gobi-desert-dunes-cliffs-camels/).
- **The Orkhon Valley and Karakorum.** Rolling green country, rivers, the site of the old imperial capital and one of the country's most important monasteries. See [our Orkhon Valley guide](/posts/orkhon-valley-karakorum/).
- **Lake Khövsgöl.** Clear water, larch forest and cooler summers in the north. See [Lake Khövsgöl in summer](/posts/lake-khovsgol-summer/).
- **The Altai.** High mountains, Kazakh culture and eagle hunting in the far west, reached most easily by a domestic flight. See [the Golden Eagle Festival](/posts/golden-eagle-festival-altai/).

If you have two weeks, our [14-day itinerary](/posts/mongolia-14-day-itinerary/) links the Gobi and the Orkhon Valley in one loop.

## Being a good guest

So much of a trip to Mongolia happens in other people's spaces: a family's ger, a monastery courtyard, a herder's pasture. A few rules and customs help.

- **Monasteries.** The FCDO asks visitors to show respect, to ask permission before taking photographs and not to touch sacred images or objects.
- **Cameras.** It is illegal to photograph or film police, police escorts or military personnel.
- **Wildlife.** It is illegal to buy, sell, kill or capture protected species or trade in their parts without a licence, so be careful with souvenirs.
- **In a ger.** Your guide will show you the small courtesies of a family home, from where to sit to how to accept a bowl of tea. Our guide to [staying with a nomad family](/posts/staying-with-nomad-family-ger/) covers them in detail.

## Why go with a local operator

You can travel independently in Mongolia, but the country's scale makes local knowledge unusually valuable. A good operator handles the things that are hard to arrange from abroad: an experienced driver and a reliable vehicle, fuel and supplies, camps and family stays, the registration your hotel would otherwise do, and the flexibility to change the route when a river is high or a road is closed. They can also tell you which festival dates are confirmed for your year, which matters because many dates are set annually.

When you choose a company, ask how long they have worked in the regions you want to visit, who your driver and guide will be, what happens if a vehicle breaks down far from town, and how they work with the herding families you will stay with. Then let them handle the logistics, and leave yourself free to look up at the sky.
