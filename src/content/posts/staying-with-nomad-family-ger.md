The jeep stops where the track gives out, and for a moment the only sound is the engine ticking as it cools. Then the dogs notice you. Somewhere beyond the sheep pen a child calls out, a door painted orange swings open, and a woman in a deel steps into the light with her hand shading her eyes. Behind her the ger sits low and white on the grass, a thin line of smoke rising from the stovepipe. Your guide climbs out first, calls a greeting and waits. You wait too. This is how it begins: slowly, on the family's terms, with nobody hurrying anybody.

A night or two in a herding family's ger is the experience most people remember longest from Mongolia. It is not a show and it is not a hotel. It is somebody's home, set up where the grazing is good this season, and you are a guest in the middle of a working day. Go in with that in mind and it can be one of the warmest welcomes you will ever receive.

## What a ger actually is

The round felt tent you will sleep in has been refined over a very long time, and it is recognised for it. UNESCO inscribed the [traditional craftsmanship of the Mongol ger and its associated customs](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/traditional-craftsmanship-of-the-mongol-ger-and-its-associated-customs-00872) on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013.

UNESCO's description reads like a set of instructions for what you are about to see. The ger has a painted and decorated wooden frame, white felt and canvas covers, ropes made from animal hair, and hand-sewn felt flooring and carpets. It is light enough for a nomadic family to carry, folds down for packing, and can be taken apart and rebuilt as the family moves. It is built to stand up to strong spring winds. The basic structure is the same across the country.

The making of it is a household affair. According to UNESCO, men usually carve the woodwork, while women and men both paint, sew, stitch and make the felt. Skills pass down mainly through older craftspeople, and children learn by watching their families take the ger down and put it up again. The people who make gers are highly respected in herding communities.

Once you know this, the inside of the tent looks different. The lattice walls, the roof poles running up to the crown, the felt layers held in place with rope: it is a portable house designed to be lived in by a family who will move it, and every part has a reason.

## Arriving: the first half hour

Your guide will usually do the talking at first. Let them. Stand back from the dogs, which are working animals guarding the herd rather than pets, and wait to be invited towards the door. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/country/151/mongolia) notes that rabies has been reported in domestic animals in Mongolia and that most cases worldwide follow a bite or scratch from an infected dog. Its advice is to avoid contact with all animals, however friendly they seem. If you are bitten, scratched or licked on broken skin, clean the wound and get urgent medical assessment, even if it looks trivial.

Inside, you will probably be offered a seat and a bowl of tea before anything else happens. Accept it, sit where your host shows you, and let the conversation find its own pace. Expect questions about where you come from, whether you have children, what your country's animals are like. Photographs of your home, your family or your own landscape travel well here, and they give everyone something to point at when words run out.

## Ger etiquette, briefly

Nobody expects visitors to know everything, and families are forgiving of honest mistakes. A few habits show respect, though, and your guide will prompt you if needed. Steppe & Sky's [guest guidelines](https://steppeandsky.tourground.com/responsible-travel) sum up the ones visitors most often ask about:

- **Do** accept tea, food or a bowl of airag with your right hand, or with both hands.
- **Do** move clockwise inside the ger, and sit where your host shows you.
- **Do** ask before photographing people, the family altar or family photographs.
- **Do** walk clockwise around ovoo, the stone cairns you will see on passes and hilltops.
- **Don't** step on the threshold of the door, or lean on the central posts.
- **Don't** point your feet at the stove or the family altar.
- **Don't** whistle inside, or throw rubbish into the fire.
- **Don't** touch someone's hat, or walk between the stove and an elder.
- **Don't** fly a drone near herds, homes or monasteries without permission.

That list looks long written down. In practice it comes down to two ideas: treat the stove, the altar and the elders with care, and when you are not sure what to do, watch your hosts and follow.

## The rhythm of a herding day

The day inside a ger is shaped by the animals outside it. Depending on the season and the family, you may see goats and sheep driven out to graze and brought home again, horses caught for riding, animals milked, dung collected and dried for fuel, and dairy worked into the cheeses, cream and curds that sit drying on the roof. Not every family keeps the same animals, and not every day looks the same.

You can ask to help, and you may well be handed a job. You may also be politely told to sit down. Either is fine. The point of the stay is to see ordinary life, not to have chores staged for you, and Steppe & Sky's guidelines make the same point: guests, not spectators, and herding life comes first.

Evenings are when the ger feels most like a home. The stove is fed, a pot goes on, the light through the crown fades, and someone may produce a deck of cards, a set of anklebones for games, or a phone full of photographs of cousins in the city. Bring patience and curiosity and the hours fill themselves.

## Eating and drinking with a clear conscience

Food in a herding household is built around meat and dairy, and you will be offered both. Eat what you are comfortable with, take small portions to begin with, and do not worry about leaving a little in the bowl.

Dairy needs a moment's thought. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/country/151/mongolia) advises that travellers should not eat or drink any unpasteurised milk products, and notes that tick-borne encephalitis can occasionally be caught from raw milk or dairy products from infected animals. Much of what you will be offered in a ger, including airag, the fermented mare's milk of summer, is made at home from fresh milk. How you handle that is your call. Many visitors accept the bowl with the right hand, raise it, and return it after a token sip or without drinking; your guide can tell you how to decline politely. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant or have a weakened immune system, take the health advice seriously and talk to a travel health professional before you go.

General food and water hygiene matters too. Carry hand sanitiser, drink boiled or treated water, and bring any prescription medicine you need from home. The [FCDO's health advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/health) says, at the time of writing (November 2026), that it is best to bring prescription medication and a first aid kit, because medicines in local pharmacies are mainly from China and Russia and may be hard to recognise.

## Practical things to sort before you go

**Who registers you.** Foreign visitors must be registered with the authorities. According to the [FCDO's entry requirements page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/entry-requirements) at the time of writing (November 2026), a hotel or landlord registers you using an app, but if you are travelling independently or staying with friends you must register yourself with the Mongolian Immigration Agency, which does not require a visit in person. If your family stay is part of an organised trip, ask your operator who is handling registration so nothing falls through the gap.

**Carry your passport.** The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/safety-and-security) says police will not accept a copy and you could be fined. Keep copies of the photo page and your entry stamp separately.

**Expect no signal.** The FCDO notes that there are large areas of rural Mongolia without network coverage. Tell people at home when you will be out of contact, download what you need beforehand, and do not plan to work.

**Remote means remote.** The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mongolia/health) warns that if you are injured or fall ill in a remote area it may be a long journey to medical care, that healthcare outside Ulaanbaatar is limited, and that hospitals require 80% of treatment costs to be paid in advance. Make sure your insurance covers medical evacuation.

**Pack for the night, not the day.** Even in summer, nights on the steppe can be cold, and the stove will be allowed to die down while everyone sleeps. A good sleeping bag or liner, a warm hat and a head torch make all the difference. Our [packing guide](/posts/what-to-pack-mongolia-weather/) covers the rest.

**Bring a small gift.** A modest, useful gift for the household is a kind gesture. Your guide will know what would be welcome for this particular family; ask before you shop rather than guessing.

## Being a guest worth having back

The families who host visitors do so alongside the real work of keeping animals alive through a hard climate. The best thing you can do is make your visit easy to accommodate.

Keep groups small. Arrive when you said you would. Agree any payment in advance through your operator so that nobody has to raise it awkwardly at the door; Steppe & Sky's guidelines describe agreeing a fair fee beforehand and never turning up unannounced. Ask before every photograph, and accept a no. Take all your rubbish away with you, including batteries and wet wipes, because there is no collection on the steppe. Buy felt, cashmere or crafts directly from the people who make them if you want to take something home.

And give it time. A single night can feel like a visit; two nights start to feel like staying. On the second morning you know where the water is kept, the children have decided you are harmless, and the dog has stopped barking at you. That is usually when the best conversations happen.

## Fitting a family stay into your trip

A ger stay works almost anywhere in the country and pairs naturally with longer journeys. In central Mongolia it sits well alongside a visit to [the Orkhon Valley and Karakorum](/posts/orkhon-valley-karakorum/), a landscape UNESCO recognises precisely for its living tradition of nomadic herding. Further north it can be combined with [Lake Khövsgöl](/posts/lake-khovsgol-summer/), and many riders spend nights with families on a [beginner's horse trek](/posts/horse-trekking-mongolia-beginners/). If you are still sketching your route, our [14-day itinerary](/posts/mongolia-14-day-itinerary/) shows one way to build a family stay into a first trip.

However you fit it in, the stay itself is simple. A felt house, a stove, a family, and a long evening under a sky that seems to start at your feet.
